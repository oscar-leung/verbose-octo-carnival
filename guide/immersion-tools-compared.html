Language Reactor vs Migaku vs Anki vs Serifu: Which Immersion Tool Fits How You Study?

"Immersion tool" covers four products that do genuinely different jobs. The honest starting point isn't "which is best?" but "which part of the work are you avoiding?" If it's reading, your answer is different than if it's speaking. Here's each tool on its own terms, then a pick per study style.

Language Reactor — the reading layer for streaming

A browser extension that turns Netflix and YouTube into a reading environment: dual subtitles, instant word popups, click-to-replay a line, auto-pause after each subtitle. For sheer frictionlessness — install it and your next episode is already study material — nothing here matches it, and the free tier covers most of what matters.

Real strengths: zero setup on content you already stream; excellent for building reading and listening comprehension during input; a generous free tier.

What it doesn't do: it's a solo, input-side tool. There is no speaking practice, no feedback on your output, and no way to study with another person. It also lives inside supported platforms — your own video files aren't its territory.

Choose it if: you study alone on Netflix or YouTube and your current bottleneck is understanding, not speaking. For that person, Language Reactor is the better pick over Serifu, full stop.

Migaku — the sentence-mining pipeline

A subscription toolchain built around one workflow: while you watch, you flag a sentence and Migaku produces a study card — audio, screenshot, sentence, definitions — in seconds. If you believe in sentence mining (and the results of people who do it consistently are hard to argue with), Migaku removes almost all of the clerical work that makes miners quit.

Real strengths: the fastest card-creation pipeline in the space; strong Japanese-specific support (parsing, pitch-accent info); an integrated ecosystem so you're not gluing tools together yourself.

What it doesn't do: it's an input-capture tool. Nothing listens to you speak; there's no group or sync layer; and it's the priciest option here, which only pays off if you actually review what you mine.

Choose it if: you're a committed solo immersion learner whose bottleneck is vocabulary volume, and you want mining friction near zero badly enough to pay for it. Again — that's a better fit than Serifu.

Anki — the memory engine everything feeds

Free, open-source spaced repetition, twenty years of community decks and add-ons, and the review algorithm most other tools imitate. Anki isn't really a competitor to the other three — it's the destination their output flows into, and it will outlive all of us. If a card is in Anki and you do your reviews, you will remember it. That's the whole promise, and it keeps it.

Real strengths: unmatched long-term retention per minute of review; free; your data is yours forever; infinitely customizable.

What it doesn't do: no video, no audio capture, no social layer, and a famously utilitarian learning curve. It tests recall, not spoken delivery — you can know a card cold and still stumble saying it at speed (more on that gap in our sentence-SRS guide).

Choose it if: always, honestly — as the review layer. Anki belongs alongside whichever other tool you pick, which is why Serifu exports its wordbook to Anki CSV rather than trying to replace it.

Serifu — the speaking layer, with friends

Our tool, so hold this section to the same standard as the others. Serifu is a watch-party rehearsal room: friends join, playback stays in sync on everyone's screen, a furigana script scrolls alongside, each person claims a character, and rehearsal mode pauses at your line, listens, and scores your attempt 0–100 before the scene resumes. It's bring-your-own-media (your video never leaves your device; you supply the subtitle file), free, and accountless. Solo practice works too, but the social loop is the point: the tools above all serve a solo pipeline, and none of them touches spoken output or studying together.

What it doesn't do: Serifu is not a reading tool, not a sentence miner, and young next to the others. Its speech scoring checks what you said, not your pitch accent — we wrote an honest explainer on exactly what that score can and can't tell you. And it asks more of you: you bring your own media and, ideally, a friend.

Choose it if: your bottleneck is speaking — you understand plenty but freeze when it's your turn to talk — or you want studying to be something you do with people instead of a solo grind you keep abandoning.

Picks by study style

  • Solo Netflix watcher, working on comprehension: Language Reactor.
  • Committed solo miner building vocabulary: Migaku, with Anki (or Migaku's own SRS) as the review layer.
  • On a budget, review-focused: Anki plus free subtitles — slowest setup, best cost-per-retained-word.
  • Stuck at "I understand but can't speak": Serifu, and keep Anki for review.
  • You and a friend both learning: Serifu — this is the one job none of the others attempts.

These combine more than they compete. A realistic stack: input with Language Reactor or mining with Migaku, review in Anki, and a weekly speaking session where the lines finally come out of your mouth.
